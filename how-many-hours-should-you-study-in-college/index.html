How Many Hours a Week Should You Study in College?

What the federal credit hour definition implies about weekly study time, with the math for 12 and 15 credits and a plan for spreading the hours out.

Study SmarterOct 11, 2026 · 4 min read

"How much should I be studying?" is one of the most common questions new college students ask, and the usual answers range from vague ("a lot") to oddly specific numbers with no source. There is a better starting point: the US Department of Education's own definition of a credit hour, which spells out how much work a credit is supposed to represent.

What a credit hour is supposed to mean

Federal regulations at 34 CFR 600.2 define a credit hour as an amount of student work defined by an institution, approved by its accrediting agency or state, that reasonably approximates not less than:

  • one hour of classroom or direct faculty instruction, and
  • a minimum of two hours of out-of-class student work

each week for approximately fifteen weeks, for one semester hour of credit. For a quarter-hour credit, the same weekly pattern applies over ten to twelve weeks.

That is the source of the familiar "two hours outside class for every hour in class" rule. It is not folk wisdom. It is how the federal definition describes the minimum amount of work a credit should reasonably approximate.

The math for a typical course load

Using that definition, each semester credit adds about 3 hours of work per week: 1 in class and 2 outside.

Credits In class per week Outside class per week Total per week
3 (one course) 3 hours 6 hours 9 hours
12 12 hours 24 hours 36 hours
15 15 hours 30 hours 45 hours

Why 12 and 15? Under 34 CFR 668.2, a school's minimum standard for a full-time undergraduate in a standard-term credit-hour program must be at least 12 semester or quarter hours per term. Many students take 15 credits per semester to stay on pace for a degree in four years, which puts the workload at about 45 hours a week by this definition. That is roughly the size of a full-time job.

Over a 15-week semester, 15 credits works out to 45 x 15 = 675 hours of total work, or about 45 hours for each credit.

Read the definition carefully

A few details keep this from becoming a rigid rule:

  • It is an approximation and a minimum. The regulation says the work should reasonably approximate "not less than" this amount.
  • Schools define it. The credit hour is defined by each institution, and the regulation lets schools account for different delivery methods, academic calendars, disciplines, and degree levels.
  • Labs and other formats count differently. The definition allows an equivalent amount of work for laboratory work, internships, studio work, and similar activities, so a lab course may have more contact hours and less homework.
  • Out-of-class work is more than studying. Reading, problem sets, papers, and projects all count toward the two hours.

So treat 2 hours per credit per week as a planning baseline. A tough course may need more. A familiar subject may need less. Your syllabus and professor are the best guide for a specific class.

Turning the number into a weekly plan

Thirty hours of out-of-class work for 15 credits sounds like a lot until you divide it. Spread over six days, it is 5 hours a day. Spread over seven, it is about 4.3 hours a day.

A practical way to plan:

  1. List your courses and credits. Multiply each by 2 for a starting estimate of weekly outside work. A 4-credit science course gets 8 hours; a 3-credit seminar gets 6.
  2. Block the time on a calendar, the same way classes are blocked. Put each course's hours in specific slots, ideally close to that course's lecture days.
  3. Split long blocks. Two 1.5-hour sessions on different days usually work better than one 3-hour session, because spreading review across days supports retention.
  4. Track what actually happens for two weeks. Write down the hours you really spend per course. Compare them with the estimate and your grades.
  5. Adjust per course. Move hours from the course where you are comfortable to the one where you are struggling.

What if you work or have other commitments?

The credit hour math makes one thing clear: a 15-credit load plus a job of 20 hours a week adds up to about 65 hours of scheduled commitments. If that does not fit, the options are to reduce credits, reduce work hours, or accept that something will give. It is better to see that tradeoff on paper at the start of a semester than in week ten.

Key takeaways

  • Federal regulations define one semester credit hour as roughly 1 hour of instruction plus at least 2 hours of outside work per week for about 15 weeks.
  • That puts a 15-credit semester at about 45 hours of total work per week, and 12 credits at about 36.
  • The definition is a minimum approximation that each school applies, so individual courses can differ.
  • Plan by multiplying each course's credits by 2 for weekly outside hours, then block that time on a calendar.
  • Track your real hours for two weeks and move time toward the courses that need it most.
