The Cornell Note-Taking Method Explained

A step-by-step look at the five-part Cornell note-taking system from Cornell University's Learning Strategies Center, and how each step is used.

Study SmarterOct 9, 2026 · 4 min read

Most note-taking advice boils down to "write more" or "write less," which is not very useful on its own. The Cornell Note-Taking System, developed at Cornell University, is more specific: it gives notes a fixed layout and a five-step process for turning raw notes into something you can actually study from later.

Where the method comes from

Cornell University's Learning Strategies Center states that the Cornell Note-Taking System was originally developed by Cornell education professor Walter Pauk, who outlined the method in his book "How to Study in College." The Center still teaches the method today as part of its study skills resources, and makes the original one-page handout available as a PDF.

The page layout

Before the five steps, the method starts with how the page itself is divided. According to the Learning Strategies Center's PDF, a standard page is split into three areas:

  • A narrow cue column on the left, about 2.5 inches wide
  • A wider note-taking column on the right, about 6 inches wide
  • A summary strip across the bottom of the page, about 2 inches tall

This layout is what makes the later steps possible. The cue column stays empty during class and gets filled in afterward; the note-taking column is where the bulk of the lecture or reading gets captured in the moment; the summary strip forces a short synthesis once the page is done.

The five steps

The Learning Strategies Center's materials lay out the method as five steps, each tied to a specific point in the study process rather than all happening during class.

1. Record. During the lecture or while reading, use the note-taking column to capture the material using telegraphic sentences, meaning short, stripped-down phrases rather than full sentences copied word for word.

2. Questions. As soon as possible after class, the official guidance says to formulate questions in the cue column based on what is in the note-taking column. The reasoning given is specific: writing questions clarifies meanings, reveals relationships, establishes continuity, and strengthens memory, and it also sets up material in a format you will use later when studying for an exam.

3. Recite. Cover the note-taking column with a sheet of paper. Looking only at the questions or cue words in the left column, say the answers out loud in your own words, based on the facts or ideas those cue words point to. This step is where recall actually gets tested, rather than just re-reading notes.

4. Reflect. The official material frames this as asking yourself a set of questions about the material itself: what is the significance of these facts, what principle are they based on, how can I apply them, how do they fit with what I already know, and what is beyond them. This step is about connecting new notes to things you already understand, not just restating them.

5. Review. The Learning Strategies Center recommends spending at least ten minutes every week reviewing all previous notes, stating that doing so helps you retain material both for current use and for the exam later.

Why the structure matters more than the writing style

A detail worth noticing: the Learning Strategies Center's own page on note-taking points out that research shows taking notes by hand is more effective than typing on a laptop, and recommends trying different note-taking systems to see what fits a given situation, rather than treating Cornell Notes as the only correct method.

What makes Cornell Notes distinct from "just take good notes" advice is that the question-writing step and the weekly review step are built into the system itself, rather than left as a vague intention. The cue column exists specifically so that, days or weeks later, you can cover your original notes and quiz yourself using only the questions, which turns the page into a self-made practice test rather than a static record.

A simple way to start using it

If you are trying this for the first time, it helps to separate the steps across time rather than trying to do all five during class:

  • During class or reading: only do step 1 (Record). Do not try to write questions yet.
  • Within a day of class: do step 2 (Questions), writing cue-column questions while the material is still fresh.
  • When studying later: do steps 3 and 4 (Recite and Reflect) using the cue column to test yourself.
  • Once a week: do step 5 (Review), going back over all your pages from that week, not just the newest one.

Key takeaways

  • The Cornell Note-Taking System was developed by Walter Pauk and is still taught by Cornell University's Learning Strategies Center.
  • The page is split into a cue column, a note-taking column, and a summary strip, each with a different job.
  • The five steps are Record, Questions, Recite, Reflect, and Review, and they are meant to happen at different times, not all during class.
  • The cue column's purpose is to let you later cover your notes and quiz yourself using only your own questions.
  • The Learning Strategies Center recommends at least ten minutes of weekly review across all your notes, not just rereading before an exam.
